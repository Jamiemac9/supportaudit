Risk management plansthat pass a support audit.

A risk management plan is where a risk score turns into something staff actually do. This is the checklist I use on risk assessments and plans, with a weak and a strong example, and a check you can run on five files this week.

Key takeaways

  • A risk management plan passes an audit when someone who's never met the resident can see what the risk is, what's being done about it, who's doing it and when it'll be looked at again.
  • Every risk above your threshold needs a current, dated plan, and the score has to match what the rest of the file says.
  • The draft licensing regulations don't contain a risk assessment condition. The risk material sits in the proposed National Supported Housing Standards, which aren't published or in force.
  • The government response says licensing guidance will tell councils not to routinely ask for support plans. So the file has to stand up when someone does ask.

What a risk management plan is

A risk management plan is the part of a support file that says how a risk is going to be reduced. The risk assessment names and scores each risk to and from the resident. The plan takes every risk above your threshold and sets out what happens next, who does it and when it's checked again.

When I read a file, I put three things side by side: the risk assessment, the plan and the last month of session notes. If the assessment says a risk is high and nothing else in the file changes, that's the gap. It's rarely that nobody cared. It's usually that the plan was written once and never touched again.

The checklist

This is what I'd look for in every risk assessment and plan. You don't need a long document to pass any of it.

  1. The risk is described for this resident. "Risk to self" on its own tells nobody anything. Say what the risk looks like for this person, and where the evidence for it is in the file.
  2. The score matches the file. Use your own matrix. But if the notes describe two incidents last month and the score hasn't moved, an auditor will ask why.
  3. Every risk above your threshold has a plan. Count them. The number of risks scored above your threshold should match the number of current plans.
  4. Each action is specific. "Monitor" isn't an action. "Key worker checks the flat with the resident at each weekly session" is.
  5. Each action has a named owner. A role or a name, plus who covers when that person is on leave.
  6. The resident's view is recorded. What they think of the risk and the plan, or why it couldn't be recorded.
  7. There's a review date, and it's been met. And the plan is looked at again straight after an incident, not only at the next scheduled review.
  8. The plan shows up in the notes. Session notes should show the actions happening. A plan nobody mentions again is the most common thing an auditor finds.

A weak plan and a strong plan

These are invented examples, written to show the difference. They're not from any real file.

Weak: "Risk: self-neglect. Score: 12 (high). Plan: staff to monitor and support. Review: ongoing."

An auditor can't tell what self-neglect means for this person, what anyone will actually do, who'll do it, or when anyone will check whether it's working.

Strong: "Risk: resident hasn't been eating regularly since losing their job, and on two visits this month there was no food in the flat (see session notes). Score: 12, high (likelihood 4, severity 3). Actions agreed with the resident: weekly food shop together on Mondays; referral to the local food bank; key worker checks the kitchen with the resident's agreement at each session. Owner: key worker, team leader covers leave. Resident's view: says the main problem is money until their benefit claim is sorted, and is happy with the weekly shop. Review: in 2 weeks, or straight away if two sessions are missed."

The second one is longer, but every part of it can be checked. A worker knows what to do on Monday, and an auditor can follow each action into the notes.

What the rules actually say

This one catches a lot of people out. The draft Supported Housing (Licensing) (England) Regulations 2027, out for consultation until 10 December 2026, don't contain a risk assessment condition. The word "risk" appears once, in a definition of who supported housing is for, not as a requirement.

What the draft licence conditions in Schedule 3 do propose is that the housing is safe, well maintained and accessible (paragraph 13), that each resident's needs are assessed no later than 4 weeks after they move in (paragraph 14), and that the housing and its support meet any national standards published under section 3 of the 2023 Act (paragraphs 11 and 17).

The risk material is in the proposed standards. The government response to the supported housing regulation consultation, first published on 16 April 2026 and updated on 16 October 2026, describes an environment standard that expects "risk management processes are in place", alongside arrangements for managing referrals so people are safe and residents aren't placed in an inappropriate mix. It lists a "risk assessment policy and procedure" as additional evidence. The staff and safeguarding standard lists "logs of safeguarding incidents and the actions taken in response" and "records of safeguarding referrals made to local authorities or other relevant agencies". And where a service takes emergency referrals, "they will be expected to have a policy in place to manage the associated risk".

To be clear about where this stops: the National Supported Housing Standards haven't been published and aren't in force, and the draft regulations are a draft. If the standards come out in the form the response describes, a risk management plan is one of the places they'd be evidenced.

Who gets to read it

The same response says MHCLG's licensing guidance will set out that support plans must not be routinely requested, because they may contain special category data and may be about people at risk of harm, giving domestic abuse survivors as an example.

Risk records are often the most sensitive part of a file. That cuts both ways. The plan needs to be complete enough to stand up when someone with a good reason asks for it. And it shouldn't be travelling around in emails, shared folders or tools nobody has checked. Keep it in the file, and keep a note of who has had a copy.

Self-check: would your plans stand up?

Pull five files from different workers, with the risk assessment, the risk management plan and the last month of notes for each, and ask:

  • Is every risk described in this resident's situation, with the evidence in the file?
  • Does every score match what the notes describe?
  • Does every risk above your threshold have a current, dated plan?
  • Does every action have a named owner?
  • Is the resident's view recorded?
  • Was the plan reviewed after the last incident?
  • Do the notes show the actions happening?

For how risk sits in the rest of the file, including a common scoring banding, see the resident support file guide. For how each note should record a risk and its next action, see how to write case notes that pass a support audit, and for linking actions to the resident's goals, see individual support plan goals that pass an audit.

Three changes you could make this week

  • Add two fields to every row of your risk management plan template: "named owner" and "review by".
  • Add one line to your incident process: after any incident, the resident's risk assessment and plan are reviewed within a set time, and write that time into the policy.
  • Run the count on five files: list every risk above your threshold and tick off the ones with a current, dated plan.
