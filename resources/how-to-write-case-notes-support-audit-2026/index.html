How to write case notesthat pass a support audit.

The session note is where most support files are won or lost. This guide sets out what a note needs to show, with a weak and a strong example, and a check you can run on ten notes this afternoon.

Key takeaways

  • A session note passes an audit when someone who was not there can tell what happened, why it mattered and what happens next.
  • Six things: date and times, who was there, what was done, the support plan goal, any risk raised, and the next action with an owner.
  • The link to a support plan goal is the part most often missing. Without it, the file cannot show the support matches the resident's needs.
  • The draft licensing regulations propose a needs assessment within 4 weeks of a resident moving in. Notes are where the file shows that assessment being acted on.

Why the session note matters

If you want to know how to write case notes that stand up, start with who reads them. A support worker writes a note for the next shift. An auditor reads it months later, with no memory of the resident and nothing else to go on. If the note does not say it, as far as the file is concerned it did not happen.

The assessment and the support plan say what a resident needs. The session notes are the only record that support was actually given. When I read a file, the notes are where I spend most of my time, because that is where the plan either turns into action or quietly stops.

What a session note needs

A note that passes has six things in it:

  1. The date, start time and end time. Not just the date. Times show the session took place and how long it lasted.
  2. Who was there. The worker, the resident, and anyone else, such as a family member or another agency.
  3. What was done. A sentence or two of fact. What was discussed, what was completed, what the resident said they would do.
  4. Which support plan goal it relates to. Name the goal, in the same words the plan uses.
  5. Any risk or concern raised. Including anything about the property, and what was done with it.
  6. The next action and who owns it. With a date where there is one.

None of this needs a long note. Most good notes are five to ten lines.

A weak note and a strong note

These are invented examples, written to show the difference. They are not from any real file.

Weak: "Met with resident. All fine. Discussed money. Will see next week."

An auditor cannot tell when the session happened, how long it lasted, what "discussed money" means, whether it relates to anything in the plan, or what is meant to happen next.

Strong: "10:15 to 10:50, resident's room. Worker and resident present. Goal 2 (manage rent and bills): went through this month's bank statement together. Resident has two missed gas top-ups and said the meter keeps running low. Agreed to call the energy supplier together on Thursday to ask about a payment plan. Resident said the bedroom window will not close properly; reported to repairs at 11:00, reference given to resident. Next session Thursday 14:00, worker to bring supplier details."

The second note is not much longer. It shows the support was planned, linked to a goal, acted on, and followed up. It also shows a property issue going somewhere, which matters more now that hazards raised with support staff can start legal deadlines in some schemes.

Linking the note to a plan goal

The goal link is the one most often missing, and it is the one that carries the most weight. A file can have weekly notes for a year and still not show that the support matched what the resident needed, because nothing ties the notes back to the plan. For how to write goals that a note can point at, see individual support plan goals that pass an audit.

This thread is likely to matter more under licensing. The draft Supported Housing (Licensing) (England) Regulations 2027 propose that a licence holder must make sure each resident's needs are assessed no later than 4 weeks after they start living in the housing (Schedule 3, paragraph 14), by a suitably skilled person and in line with any national standards published under section 3 of the 2023 Act (paragraph 15). The regulations are a draft and the consultation closes on 10 December 2026. Nothing in them is in force.

Section 3 of the Supported Housing (Regulatory Oversight) Act 2023 lets the Secretary of State publish National Supported Housing Standards, and says they may set minimum standards for the provision of care, support or supervision. If standards on support are published, the file is how a provider would show it meets them. An assessment says what is needed, the plan turns that into goals, and the notes show the goals being worked on. Break the chain at the notes and the rest cannot be evidenced.

Self-check: would your notes stand up?

Pull ten recent session notes from different workers and ask:

  • Does every note have a start and end time, not just a date?
  • Could someone who was not there tell what was actually done?
  • Does every note name a support plan goal, in the plan's own words?
  • Is every risk or property issue followed by what was done about it?
  • Does every note end with a next action and a named owner?
  • Does the next note pick up that action?

If fewer than eight of the ten pass every question, the fix is usually a short note template and ten minutes in team meetings, not more paperwork. For what the rest of the file should contain, see the resident support file guide, and for how hazards in notes should reach repairs, see Awaab's Law Phase 2 and support records.
