Individual support plan goalsthat pass an audit.

The support plan is where a needs assessment turns into something a worker can act on. This guide sets out what each goal needs to show, with a weak and a strong example, and a check you can run on five plans this week.

Key takeaways

  • A support plan goal passes an audit when someone who has never met the resident can tell what need it answers, what it is for, what support is given and whether it is working.
  • Four things: the assessed need, an outcome the resident agreed, the level and type of support with who gives it, and a review that records progress and barriers.
  • The government's proposed person-centred support standard expects a tailored support plan with agreed outcomes. The standards are not yet published or in force.
  • A goal that cannot be traced back to the needs assessment, or forward to the session notes, is the gap an auditor finds first.

What the plan is for

An individual support plan is the document that says what support a resident will get, and why. The needs assessment says what the resident needs. The plan turns each need into a goal. The session notes show the goal being worked on. If the plan is vague, everything after it is hard to evidence, however good the support was.

When I read a file, I read the plan with the assessment open next to it. A plan is rarely weak because support was missing. It is weak because the goals are too general to show what the support was for.

What a goal needs

A goal that passes has four things in it:

  1. The need it answers. Name the need from the assessment, in the same words. If a goal does not match an assessed need, an auditor will ask why it is there. If an assessed need has no goal, they will ask why not.
  2. An outcome the resident agreed. What will be different, in plain words the resident would recognise. Record that they agreed it, or why they could not.
  3. The support, and who gives it. The level and type of support: how often, what kind, and which worker or agency. "Weekly 30 minute session with key worker" is something a note can be checked against. "Ongoing support" is not.
  4. A review. A date to look at it again, and a record of what has changed, what is working and what is getting in the way.

None of this needs a long plan. Three or four goals done properly are worth more than ten that nobody can measure.

A weak goal and a strong goal

These are invented examples, written to show the difference. They are not from any real file.

Weak: "Goal: maintain tenancy. Support: ongoing support with tenancy matters."

An auditor cannot tell which need this answers, what maintaining the tenancy looks like for this person, how much support is given or by whom, or when anyone will check whether it is working.

Strong: "Need (assessment, section 3): rent arrears from a previous tenancy and no bank account. Goal agreed with resident: open a bank account and set up a repayment plan for the old arrears, so rent can be paid by standing order. Support: weekly 30 minute session with key worker, help to gather ID, attend bank appointment together, referral to a free debt advice service. Review: in 6 weeks. Progress and anything in the way recorded at each session."

The second goal is longer, but each part can be checked. A worker knows what to do on Monday, and an auditor can follow it into the notes.

What the proposed standard says

The government response to the supported housing regulation consultation, first published on 16 April 2026 and updated on 16 October 2026, describes a proposed person-centred support standard. Among its expectations:

  • "a tailored support and/or supervision plan with agreed outcomes must be in place to accompany the individual needs assessment"
  • the plan "must set out the level and type of support required to meet the identified need(s) of the resident"
  • "wherever possible the resident or their advocate has worked with the support provider to design their support and/or supervision plan"
  • "achieving agreed outcomes is demonstrated and barriers to achieving them is identified"

The same response lists "support reviews" as additional evidence, and says "Standardised needs assessments and support plans will not be mandated, but there will be criteria that needs assessments and support plans must include." So providers keep their own formats, but the content will be checked.

Two things to be clear about. The National Supported Housing Standards have not been published and are not in force. And the draft Supported Housing (Licensing) (England) Regulations 2027, out for consultation until 10 December 2026, do not use the phrase "support plan" at all. What the draft does propose is a needs assessment within 4 weeks of a resident moving in (Schedule 3, paragraph 14), carried out in line with any national standards published under section 3 of the 2023 Act (paragraph 15), and that support or supervision not provided by a registered care provider meets any such standards (paragraph 17). If the standards are published in the form the response describes, the plan is where that would be evidenced.

Property and support side by side

The draft licence conditions put property and support in the same schedule. Paragraph 13 proposes the housing must be safe, well maintained and accessible for its intended residents. Paragraphs 14 to 17 cover needs assessments and support. A council checking one is likely to be looking at the other in the same visit.

Most property files already run on dates: a certificate, an expiry, a named contractor, a next check. Support plans often do not. A goal with a named owner and a review date is the support file doing what the property file has done for years.

Self-check: would your plans stand up?

Pull five current support plans from different workers, with the needs assessment for each, and ask:

  • Does every assessed need have a goal, and does every goal match an assessed need?
  • Is each outcome written so the resident would recognise it?
  • Does the plan record that the resident agreed it, or why not?
  • Does each goal say how often support is given and by whom?
  • Does each goal have a review date, and has the review happened?
  • Do the last four session notes name a goal from this plan?

If most plans fail on review dates or on who gives the support, the fix is usually two extra fields in the plan template. For how session notes should link back to each goal, see how to write case notes that pass a support audit, and for the rest of the file, see the resident support file guide.
