The ASHA inquiry.Flag the conflict.

On 3 November 2026 the Regulator of Social Housing commissioned an independent statutory inquiry into Ash-Shahada Housing Association. The scope covers conflicts of interest, third-party payments and how social housing assets are used. No findings have been published. Providers preparing for a fit and proper person test need support files and governance records that show care delivery is separate from third-party financial arrangements.

Quick brief

  • The threat. On 3 November 2026 the RSH commissioned an independent statutory inquiry into Ash-Shahada Housing Association (ASHA), which provides supported exempt accommodation in Birmingham.
  • The focus. The inquirer must report on whether ASHA, its officers, employees or agents made payments or conferred benefits to advance the interests of third parties.
  • The standard. Under incoming licensing rules, providers must pass a fit and proper person test, which demands financial integrity.
  • The action. Internal auditing has to connect resident support with governance, so conflicts of interest in care contracting are visible.

Governance failures are now audit failures

For years, operations managers and frontline auditors in the Supported Exempt Accommodation (SEA) sector have focused almost exclusively on proving that residents receive more than minimal care. On 3 November 2026 the regulatory question widened.1

The Regulator of Social Housing (RSH) commissioned an independent statutory inquiry into Ash-Shahada Housing Association (ASHA). ASHA is a London-based landlord. It provides social housing in London and supported exempt accommodation in Birmingham. The RSH called a statutory inquiry a tool it uses very rarely, and said this one reflects the severity of its concerns and a longstanding failure to put things right.1

The inquiry is not itself a finding, and the RSH has said it will publish the outcome when the inquiry concludes. The independent inquirer has to report on whether there is any evidence that ASHA, its officers, employees or agents entered into arrangements, made payments, or conferred other benefits "for the purpose of advancing the interests of any third parties".1

Auditing for conflicts of interest

The scope of the ASHA inquiry shows that the RSH will not treat resident support and corporate governance as separate issues. Local authority licensing teams are likely to take the same view. If a financial structure puts third-party landlords or agents ahead of resident outcomes, that is the weakness a fit and proper person test is there to catch.1,2

Internal auditing has to be able to see that weakness now:

  • Support provider separation. Check that the agencies or individuals contracted to deliver frontline support are independent of the property's managing agents or landlords. Record who employs them, who pays them, and who they report to.
  • Board competence. The inquiry is examining whether the board and executive have the skills, independence and effectiveness to manage the landlord's affairs. Auditors should be able to see that the people directing support are competent to do it, and that they are not marking their own homework.1
  • Asset use. Cross-reference resident support files with property records. Social housing assets should be used in line with the landlord's policies and the regulatory requirements, not for an undisclosed private benefit.1

A compliance system that only stores weekly contact logs, and cannot show who provides the care or who benefits from the contract, leaves the organisation exposed if a regulator or a licensing team asks. Read how the fit and proper person test fits the licensing regime.

Frequently asked questions

What is the RSH investigating regarding Ash-Shahada Housing Association?

The independent statutory inquiry is examining whether there are conflicts of interest, whether the board and executive have the skills and independence to manage affairs, and whether payments, benefits or social housing assets were used to advance third-party interests. No findings have been published yet.1

How does governance affect frontline support auditing?

If a provider has conflicts of interest, such as arrangements that advance a third party rather than the resident, that undermines the integrity of the support recorded in the file. A licensing team or the regulator can treat that as a failure of the provider, not only of a support note.
